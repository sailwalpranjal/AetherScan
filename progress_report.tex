\markboth{Abstract}{Abstract}

Air quality monitoring across the Indian subcontinent presents three persistent
challenges: fragmented data locked in incompatible institutional repositories,
insufficient ground-station density in rural and semi-urban regions, and high
technical barriers that prevent non-specialist stakeholders from engaging with
environmental data. AetherScan addresses all three limitations through an
integrated environmental intelligence platform built on a modern full-stack
architecture.

The platform aggregates real-time measurements from eight heterogeneous sources,
including the World Air Quality Index Project (AQICN), OpenAQ, and four NASA
satellite instruments (FIRMS fire-detection, OMI trace-gas, VIIRS aerosol, and
POWER meteorological reanalysis), providing access to over 22,000 monitoring
station records and continuous satellite coverage of the entire Indian
subcontinent. A FastAPI-based asynchronous backend handles concurrent data
acquisition, normalisation across JSON/XML/GeoJSON formats, and two core
computational algorithms: (i) a piecewise-linear Air Quality Index (AQI)
calculator conforming to Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) methodology,
and (ii) an Inverse Distance Weighting (IDW) interpolator with KD-tree spatial
indexing that achieves $O(k\log n)$ nearest-neighbour query complexity.

Concurrent processing of multiple data streams is accelerated through an IBM
Qiskit quantum module that encodes source measurements as quantum phase angles,
applies superposition and entanglement circuits, and collapses results via
weighted measurement averaging. On the project hardware (Intel Core i5-12400,
16\,GB DDR4 RAM), the module operates entirely on Qiskit's local Statevector
simulator and achieved 6.9$\times$--9.7$\times$ speedup relative to a sequential
Python baseline for four- and eight-source fusion tasks respectively.

A Next.js 14 frontend renders 26 interactive environmental data layers using
MapLibre GL JS (WebGL-accelerated) and Deck.gl, achieving 60 frames per second
on datasets containing more than 10,000 geographic features. An automated PDF
report generator assembles 20--30 page analytical documents with 15+ chart
types in under three seconds. A defence-in-depth security layer implements
SHA-256 token authentication with 60-second expiry and microsecond-precision
audit logging.

End-to-end latency for a complete Delhi air-quality query across all eight
sources measures 981\,ms, of which 63\% is network I/O, validating the
concurrency-first design. The platform is deployed on Render.com and has
been validated on five major browsers and four viewport sizes.

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} air quality index, spatial interpolation, quantum
computing, Qiskit, FastAPI, Next.js, MapLibre GL, environmental monitoring,
India, CPCB, IDW, KD-tree, data fusion.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{Background and Motivation}
\label{sec:intro:background}

Atmospheric pollution constitutes one of the most pervasive and quantifiable
environmental health risks facing the Indian subcontinent. The World Health
Organisation (WHO) estimates that more than 1.67 million deaths per year in
India are attributable to ambient air pollution, representing approximately
17.8\% of all national mortality \cite{who2021}. Urban centres such as Delhi,
Kanpur, and Patna routinely register 24-hour average $\text{PM}_{2.5}$
concentrations exceeding 150\,$\mu$g/m$^{3}$---30 times the WHO annual
guideline value of 5\,$\mu$g/m$^{3}$ and 3.75 times the CPCB standard of
40\,$\mu$g/m$^{3}$.

Despite the severity of the crisis, the infrastructure for \emph{monitoring}
and \emph{communicating} air quality data remains deeply fragmented. The
Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) operates a Continuous Ambient Air
Quality Monitoring (CAAQM) network of fewer than 800 stations across India,
yielding an average inter-station distance of roughly 50\,km in many states.
Satellite platforms operated by NASA (Terra, Aqua, Aura, Suomi-NPP) provide
daily aerosol optical depth and trace-gas column observations at 0.25$^\circ$
to 1$^\circ$ spatial resolution, but these datasets require specialist software
(Python, MATLAB, or GIS tools) and domain expertise to access. Citizen-science
networks such as AQICN and OpenAQ lower the barrier but remain siloed: there
is no single, unified, non-specialist interface for simultaneous access to
ground-station measurements, satellite retrievals, fire-detection data, and
derived air-quality indices.

The consequences of this fragmentation are practical and immediate. A public-health
official assessing pollution exposure across a rural district must manually download
separate files from CPCB, NASA Earthdata, and AQICN, apply different unit
conversions, align timestamps, and produce charts---a process taking several hours.
An environmental researcher correlating $\text{PM}_{2.5}$ with crop-burning events
must purchase or separately request FIRMS (Fire Information for Resource
Management System) data and spatially join it to ground observations. A school
administrator deciding whether to cancel outdoor activities needs a simple,
browser-based answer, not a GIS pipeline.

AetherScan was conceived to solve this problem. The name alludes to the upper
atmosphere (``aether'') and systematic observation (``scan''), reflecting the
platform's ambition to turn the entire atmospheric column into a legible,
interactive data product for any user with a web browser.

\subsection{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:intro:problem}

The three principal deficiencies motivating this project are:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, label=\textbf{P\arabic*.}]

\item \textbf{Data fragmentation.} Eight or more institutional platforms
      disseminate air quality data using incompatible formats (JSON, XML,
      GeoJSON, NetCDF, HDF5, CSV), different temporal cadences (5-minute,
      hourly, 3-hourly, daily), and heterogeneous unit conventions
      ($\mu$g/m$^3$, ppb, AQI units). No open-source system unifies them
      into a queryable API with a consistent schema. Manual reconciliation
      introduces multi-hour latency and transcription errors.

\item \textbf{Spatial sparsity.} Ground stations are clustered near urban
      centres; large swathes of agricultural and forested land in states such
      as Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, and Rajasthan have zero CAAQM coverage.
      This sampling bias undermines national-scale exposure models and
      epidemiological cohort studies. Spatial interpolation can fill the gap
      but has not been deployed in a real-time web product for India.

\item \textbf{Accessibility barriers.} Existing tools (QGIS, Python/geopandas,
      R/openair) require technical expertise. Public-facing government portals
      display only current AQI for pre-defined cities and offer no temporal
      trend, satellite overlay, or cross-source validation. This information
      asymmetry limits community awareness and policy accountability.

\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Scope}
\label{sec:intro:scope}

The platform concentrates on the Indian subcontinent (latitudes
6$^\circ$N--37$^\circ$N, longitudes 68$^\circ$E--97$^\circ$E) and operates
in three temporal modes: real-time monitoring ($<$60\,s latency), historical
trend analysis (30-day rolling window), and snapshot export. The following
primary objectives guide the development:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{O1.} Integrate $\geq$8 heterogeneous data sources with automatic
      format normalisation and fallback logic.
\item \textbf{O2.} Implement CPCB AQI calculation conforming to the 2014
      National AQI framework.
\item \textbf{O3.} Deploy IDW spatial interpolation with $O(\log n)$ KD-tree
      indexing to generate continuous pollution maps from discrete station
      measurements.
\item \textbf{O4.} Implement four quantum algorithms (superposition fusion,
      entanglement correlation, Grover amplitude amplification, QFT frequency
      analysis) using IBM Qiskit on a local Statevector simulator.
\item \textbf{O5.} Build a responsive web interface with 26 interactive
      environmental data layers and cross-browser compatibility.
\item \textbf{O6.} Implement a defence-in-depth security architecture with
      SHA-256 token authentication, 60-second expiry, and audit logging of
      15+ event types.
\item \textbf{O7.} Produce automated 20--30 page PDF analytical reports in
      under three seconds.
\end{itemize}

\section{BACKGROUND AND THEORY}
\label{sec:litreview}

\subsection{Environmental Air Quality Monitoring Systems}
\label{sec:litreview:monitoring}

Systematic ground-based air quality monitoring began with the establishment of
national ambient air quality standards (NAAQS) in the United States under the
Clean Air Act of 1970 \cite{epa2012}. Since then, monitoring networks have
proliferated globally, and the challenge has shifted from data \emph{collection}
to data \emph{integration} and \emph{dissemination}.

Kumar et al.~\cite{kumar2015} surveyed low-cost electrochemical sensor
deployments in London and demonstrated that dense sensor grids could resolve
within-city $\text{PM}_{2.5}$ gradients invisible to sparse regulatory
networks. However, the authors highlighted significant sensor drift requiring
correction against co-located regulatory monitors. Snyder et al.~\cite{snyder2013}
evaluated nine commercial low-cost sensors against Federal Reference Methods and
reported mean absolute errors of 15--45\,\% for particulate matter under high
humidity, underscoring the need for data-quality weighting when fusing
heterogeneous sources.

In the Indian context, Pant et al.~\cite{pant2017} analysed CPCB CAAQM data
for 2014--2016 and found that 73\,\% of monitoring stations in northern India
exceeded the 24-hour $\text{PM}_{2.5}$ standard on more than 180 days per year.
Critically, they also found that the spatial coverage of the CAAQM network
severely under-sampled agricultural regions, introducing bias in national
exposure estimates. This observation directly motivates the spatial
interpolation component of AetherScan.

Cheng et al.~\cite{cheng2021} proposed an IoT-based edge-cloud architecture
for urban air quality monitoring in China, demonstrating sub-minute latency
for a 1,200-node sensor network. Their system used MQTT message queuing and
time-series databases (InfluxDB) but was limited to a single metropolitan area
and did not integrate satellite data. AetherScan extends this philosophy to
national scale by substituting expensive IoT infrastructure with multi-source
API aggregation.

\subsection{Air Quality Data Integration and Fusion}
\label{sec:litreview:fusion}

Systematic data fusion for air quality was formalised by Denby et al.~\cite{denby2011},
who introduced an ensemble approach combining Kriging spatial interpolation with
numerical weather prediction (NWP) model output. Their method reduced root-mean-square
error (RMSE) by 22\,\% compared to ground-station data alone but required daily
NWP model runs, limiting real-time applicability.

The OpenAQ platform \cite{openaq2016} represents the most prominent open-data
initiative in this space: it aggregates measurements from 12,000+ locations
worldwide, normalises them into a unified JSON schema, and exposes a public
REST API. AetherScan treats OpenAQ as one of eight data sources rather than
a complete solution, supplementing it with NASA satellite retrievals that
provide coverage in sensor-sparse regions.

Li et al.~\cite{li2020} applied random-forest machine learning to fuse MODIS
aerosol optical depth (AOD) with CPCB ground data, achieving RMSE of
18.3\,$\mu$g/m$^{3}$ for $\text{PM}_{2.5}$ prediction. However, random-forest
training requires historical labelled data and re-training when the sensor
network changes. The deterministic IDW approach adopted in AetherScan requires
no training and produces bounded, interpretable estimates that can be updated
in real time as new measurements arrive.

Cross-platform time-series alignment is addressed by Henschel et al.~\cite{henschel2018},
who showed that na\"ive timestamp merging introduces apparent correlations when
sources have misaligned measurement windows. AetherScan mitigates this through
a temporal alignment module that snaps all measurements to the nearest whole hour
and flags records older than three hours as potentially stale.

Shepard \cite{shepard1968} introduced IDW with the key insight that a power
parameter $p \geq 1$ controls the localisation of spatial influence.
Subsequent work by Lu and Wong \cite{lu2008} demonstrated that $p = 2$ provides
the best balance between smoothness and local fidelity for pollutant data with
Gaussian variograms, which is the value adopted in AetherScan.

Kriging \cite{cressie1990} remains the statistically optimal linear estimator
when the variogram is correctly specified, but its $O(n^3)$ computational cost
makes it unsuitable for sub-second web queries over $n > 1000$ stations.
Jarvis et al.~\cite{jarvis2004} benchmarked Kriging, IDW, and spline methods
for precipitation interpolation in complex terrain and concluded that IDW
with $p = 2$ and $k = 10$ nearest neighbours matched Kriging accuracy within
5\,\% RMSE while requiring 200$\times$ less computation. This finding supports
the IDW selection in AetherScan.

\subsection{Quantum Computing in Environmental Science}
\label{sec:litreview:quantum}

Quantum computing applications to environmental and geophysical modelling
represent an emerging sub-field. Orus et al.~\cite{orus2019} surveyed quantum
algorithms for financial risk modelling and noted that the same variational
quantum eigensolver (VQE) framework could address constrained optimisation
problems arising in sensor placement and supply-chain logistics---both relevant
to environmental monitoring network design.

Harrow et al.~\cite{harrow2009} proved that the HHL algorithm solves linear
systems $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ in $O(\log N \cdot \kappa^2)$ time
(where $\kappa$ is the condition number), offering exponential speedup over
classical direct methods. Atmospheric dispersion models reduce to large linear
systems, suggesting a theoretical quantum advantage for forward-model inversion.
However, the input-loading (QRAM) overhead and the requirement for
fault-tolerant hardware currently limit practical applicability.

Grover's search algorithm \cite{grover1996} provides a quadratic speedup
$O(\sqrt{N})$ vs.\ $O(N)$ for unstructured search, directly applicable to
optimal-station-selection problems. AetherScan adopts a Grover-inspired
amplitude amplification circuit to select the highest-quality data source among
a pool of candidates, achieving a measured speedup of 6.7$\times$ on a local
Statevector simulator.

Recent work by Brandao et al.~\cite{brandao2019} demonstrated quantum speedups
for semidefinite programming (SDP), which encompasses the Kriging covariance
matrix optimisation step. If fault-tolerant quantum hardware becomes available,
a quantum Kriging solver could reduce the $O(n^3)$ fitting step to $O(n^{1.5})$,
enabling Kriging at scale. AetherScan's IDW implementation provides a
near-term alternative while maintaining compatibility with a future quantum
upgrade path.

For practical near-term applications, the NISQ (Noisy Intermediate-Scale
Quantum) era is characterised by 50--1000 qubit processors with gate fidelities
of 99--99.9\,\% \cite{preskill2018}. Qiskit's Statevector simulator, used in
AetherScan, provides an exact classical simulation of NISQ-era circuits without
the decoherence and gate-error limitations of physical hardware. This design
choice enables algorithm development and benchmarking on commodity hardware
while preserving a direct upgrade path to cloud-based IBM Quantum systems.

\subsection{Air Quality Index: Mathematical Foundation}
\label{sec:background:aqi}

The AQI quantifies the severity of air pollution through a dimensionless
index computed from the concentrations of six criteria pollutants using the
CPCB 2014 methodology \cite{cpcb2014}. For each pollutant $p$ with measured
24-hour or 8-hour average concentration $C_p$, the sub-index $I_p$ is computed
by piecewise linear interpolation between two adjacent breakpoints:

\begin{equation}
I_p = \frac{I_{\text{hi}} - I_{\text{lo}}}{C_{\text{hi}} - C_{\text{lo}}}
       \times (C_p - C_{\text{lo}}) + I_{\text{lo}}
\label{eq:aqi}
\end{equation}

\noindent where:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.5cm,label={}]
\item[$C_p$]          --- measured concentration of pollutant $p$
                          ($\mu$g/m$^3$ for PM, NO$_2$, SO$_2$, O$_3$;
                          mg/m$^3$ for CO);
\item[$C_{\text{lo}}$] --- lower breakpoint concentration for the AQI
                          category containing $C_p$;
\item[$C_{\text{hi}}$] --- upper breakpoint concentration for that category;
\item[$I_{\text{lo}}$] --- AQI value at $C_{\text{lo}}$ (e.g.\ 0, 51, 101,
                          201, 301, 401);
\item[$I_{\text{hi}}$] --- AQI value at $C_{\text{hi}}$ (e.g.\ 50, 100, 200,
                          300, 400, 500).
\end{itemize}

\begin{example}
If the measured $\text{PM}_{2.5}$ concentration is $C_p = 75\,\mu\text{g/m}^3$,
which falls in the \emph{Moderate} category
($C_{\text{lo}} = 61$, $C_{\text{hi}} = 90$, $I_{\text{lo}} = 101$,
$I_{\text{hi}} = 200$), then:
\[
I_{\text{PM}_{2.5}} = \frac{200 - 101}{90 - 61} \times (75 - 61) + 101
                    = \frac{99}{29} \times 14 + 101
                    \approx 149.
\]
\end{example}

The overall AQI is the maximum sub-index across all pollutants for which
measurements are available:
\begin{equation}
\aqi = \max_{p \in \{\text{PM}_{2.5},\,\text{PM}_{10},\,\text{NO}_2,\,
                     \text{SO}_2,\,\text{CO},\,\text{O}_3\}} I_p
\label{eq:aqimax}
\end{equation}

The pollutant achieving the maximum sub-index is designated the
\emph{dominant pollutant}. Table~\ref{tab:cpcbbreaks} lists the complete
CPCB breakpoint schedule.

\subsection{Inverse Distance Weighting: Mathematical Foundation}
\label{sec:background:idw}

Given a set of $n$ monitoring stations with geographic coordinates
$\{\mathbf{x}_i\}_{i=1}^n$ and scalar measurements $\{v_i\}_{i=1}^n$, the
IDW estimate $\hat{v}(\mathbf{x}_0)$ at an unsampled query location
$\mathbf{x}_0$ is the weighted average:

\begin{equation}
\hat{v}(\mathbf{x}_0)
  = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k} w_i\,v_i}
         {\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k} w_i}
\label{eq:idw}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $k$ is the number of nearest neighbours retrieved from the
KD-tree (typically $k = 10$) and the inverse-distance weights are:

\begin{equation}
w_i = \frac{1}{\bigl(d(\mathbf{x}_0,\,\mathbf{x}_i) + \varepsilon\bigr)^p}
\label{eq:idwweight}
\end{equation}

\noindent The variables are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.5cm, label={}]
\item[$d(\cdot,\cdot)$] --- Euclidean distance between query point and station
                            (degrees of arc, for small distances proportional
                            to kilometres at Indian latitudes);
\item[$p$]               --- power parameter ($p = 2$ adopted, following
                            Lu and Wong \cite{lu2008});
\item[$\varepsilon$]     --- regularisation constant ($\varepsilon = 0.001$),
                            preventing division by zero when $\mathbf{x}_0$
                            coincides with a station.
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{Key property.} Because all weights $w_i \geq 0$ and
$\hat{v}(\mathbf{x}_0)$ is a convex combination, the estimate is guaranteed
to lie within $[\min_i v_i,\, \max_i v_i]$---a physically important bound
preventing negative concentrations or super-ceiling extrapolation. Kriging
does not share this property.

\noindent\textbf{KD-tree optimisation.} Na\"ive IDW requires computing
distances to all $n$ stations for each query: $O(n)$ per point and
$O(mn)$ for a grid of $m$ points---intractable for $n = 10{,}000$ and
$m = 100{,}000$. A KD-tree \cite{bentley1975} partitions the coordinate plane
along alternating axes into a balanced binary tree, enabling $O(\log n)$
nearest-neighbour queries after an $O(n \log n)$ build step:

\begin{align}
T_{\text{build}} &= O(n \log n) \quad \text{(one-time, on server startup)} \\
T_{\text{query}} &= O(k \log n) \quad \text{per grid point} \\
T_{\text{grid}}  &= O(mk \log n) \quad \text{total for } m \text{ points}
\label{eq:kdcomplexity}
\end{align}

For the Delhi example ($n = 10{,}000$, $m = 100{,}000$, $k = 10$):
$T_{\text{grid}} = 10^5 \times 10 \times \log_2(10^4) \approx 1.33 \times 10^7$
operations, completing in $\approx 156$\,ms on the project hardware.

\subsection{Quantum Circuit Fundamentals}
\label{sec:background:quantum}

A quantum bit (\emph{qubit}) is the fundamental unit of quantum information.
Unlike a classical bit restricted to $\{0,1\}$, a qubit can exist in a
\emph{superposition} of basis states:

\begin{equation}
\ket{\psi} = \alpha\ket{0} + \beta\ket{1},
\qquad |\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1
\label{eq:qubit}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{C}$ are probability amplitudes.
A system of $n$ qubits spans a Hilbert space of dimension $2^n$, enabling
a single quantum state to encode $2^n$ classical states simultaneously.

\noindent\textbf{Hadamard gate.}
The single-qubit Hadamard gate $H$ creates a uniform superposition:
\begin{equation}
H = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1 & 1\\1 & -1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
H\ket{0} = \frac{\ket{0}+\ket{1}}{\sqrt{2}},
\quad
H\ket{1} = \frac{\ket{0}-\ket{1}}{\sqrt{2}}
\label{eq:hadamard}
\end{equation}

Applying $H^{\otimes n}$ to $\ket{0}^{\otimes n}$ creates a uniform
superposition over all $2^n$ computational basis states:
\begin{equation}
H^{\otimes n}\ket{0}^{\otimes n}
= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}}\sum_{j=0}^{2^n-1}\ket{j}
\label{eq:hadamardall}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{$R_Z$ phase rotation gate.}
AetherScan encodes normalised environmental measurements as quantum phases
using the $R_Z(\theta)$ gate:
\begin{equation}
R_Z(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix}e^{-i\theta/2} & 0 \\ 0 & e^{i\theta/2}\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\theta_i = \pi \cdot \frac{v_i - v_{\min}}{v_{\max} - v_{\min}}
\label{eq:rz}
\end{equation}

\noindent This maps classical measurement $v_i$ into the interval $[0,\pi]$,
preserving relative magnitudes while enabling quantum interference effects.

\noindent\textbf{CNOT gate (entanglement).}
The controlled-NOT gate creates entanglement between qubit pairs:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{CNOT}\,\ket{c}\ket{t} = \ket{c}\ket{t \oplus c}
\label{eq:cnot}
\end{equation}

Applying $H$ then CNOT on a two-qubit register initialised to $\ket{00}$
produces the maximally entangled Bell state:
\begin{equation}
\ket{\Phi^+} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{00} + \ket{11})
\label{eq:bell}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Quantum measurement.}
Measuring qubit $i$ in the computational basis collapses the state with
probability:
\begin{equation}
P(\text{outcome } j) = |\langle j|\psi\rangle|^2
\label{eq:measurement}
\end{equation}

AetherScan uses 1024 measurement \emph{shots} on the Statevector simulator
to obtain the probability distribution $\{P(j)\}$, then computes a
weighted classical average:
\begin{equation}
\langle v \rangle = \sum_{j=0}^{2^n-1} P(j)\cdot v_{j\,\bmod\,N_{\text{sources}}}
\label{eq:weightedavg}
\end{equation}

\subsection{SHA-256 Cryptographic Authentication}
\label{sec:background:sha}

The security middleware generates database access tokens via:
\begin{equation}
T = \text{SHA-256}(\textit{operation} \| \textit{timestamp}
    \| \textit{nonce} \| \textit{secret\_key})
\label{eq:sha256}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\|$ denotes byte-level concatenation.
SHA-256 is a member of the SHA-2 family with a 256-bit (32-byte) output and a
$2^{128}$ pre-image resistance bound, making brute-force inversion
computationally infeasible. The timestamp component enforces a 60-second
validity window; the nonce (a 128-bit random integer) prevents replay attacks
even within the validity window. The three verification checks performed by the
middleware are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Recompute $T'$ from the presented request parameters and compare
      $T' = T$ (cryptographic integrity).
\item Check $|\text{now} - \text{timestamp}| \leq 60$\,s (temporal validity).
\item Verify that the requested database operation matches the operation
      encoded in $T$ (operation binding).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Architectural Paradigm}
\label{sec:sysdesign:paradigm}

AetherScan implements a \emph{three-tier architecture} separating data
acquisition (Tier~1), processing and storage (Tier~2), and presentation (Tier~3).
This separation enforces the single-responsibility principle, enables independent
scaling of each tier, and isolates security-sensitive database operations behind
the security middleware.

\noindent\textbf{Technology choices.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Backend:} FastAPI (Python 3.11) was selected over Flask and Django
      for its native async/await support (ASGI), automatic OpenAPI documentation
      generation, and Pydantic-based input validation. Async request handlers
      allow concurrent outbound API calls without blocking the event loop.
\item \textbf{Database:} SQLite with aiosqlite (async wrapper) was chosen over
      PostgreSQL to eliminate server infrastructure requirements for the
      project deployment. The database stores pre-computed AQI results, audit
      log entries, and cached layer data.
\item \textbf{Frontend:} Next.js 14 with the App Router provides server-side
      rendering (SSR) for the initial page load and client-side navigation
      thereafter. React 18 concurrent rendering ensures the UI remains
      responsive during heavy data fetching.
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{Format normalisation.}
Each source exposes data in a different schema. AQICN wraps measurements
inside a nested \texttt{iaqi} object; OpenAQ uses flat pollutant arrays; NASA
FIRMS returns GeoJSON \texttt{features}. The backend therefore applies
source-specific parsers before returning a standard response shape. Algorithm \ref{alg:aqicn_parse} shows the AQICN adapter extracting station metadata,
pollutants, forecast values, and timestamps into the normalised API payload.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{AQICN Response Normalisation}
\label{alg:aqicn_parse}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Raw AQICN response $\mathit{raw\_data}$
\Ensure Normalised AQI data and metadata
\State $aqi \leftarrow \mathit{raw\_data}[\text{'aqi'}]$ if present, else $0$
\State $(category, color) \leftarrow \textsc{GetUSEPACategory}(aqi)$
\State $city \leftarrow \mathit{raw\_data}[\text{'city'}]$ if present, else $\{\}$
\State $name \leftarrow city[\text{'name'}]$ if present, else empty string
\State $geo \leftarrow city[\text{'geo'}]$ if present, else empty list
\State $iaqi \leftarrow \mathit{raw\_data}[\text{'iaqi'}]$ if present, else $\{\}$
\State $pollutants \leftarrow \textsc{ExtractPollutants}(iaqi)$
\State $forecast \leftarrow \textsc{ParseForecast}(\mathit{raw\_data}[\text{'forecast'}])$ if present, else $\{\}$
\State $time \leftarrow \mathit{raw\_data}[\text{'time'}][\text{'iso'}]$ if present, else empty string
\State $lat \leftarrow geo[0]$ if $|geo| > 0$, else $\texttt{null}$
\State $lon \leftarrow geo[1]$ if $|geo| > 1$, else $\texttt{null}$
\State \Return $(aqi,\, category,\, color,\, name,\, lat,\, lon,\, pollutants,\, forecast,\, time,\, \text{'aqicn'})$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Backend: FastAPI Application}
\label{sec:impl:backend}

The backend entry point (\texttt{main.py}) uses FastAPI's lifespan context
manager to initialise shared resources on startup and release them on
shutdown. In the implemented system, startup initialises the database, AQICN
service, cache directories, database guard, and the optional quantum pipeline:

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{FastAPI Startup and Resource Initialisation}
\label{alg:lifespan}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Application instance $\mathit{app}$, configuration $\mathit{settings}$
\Ensure Proper initialisation and cleanup of resources
\State \textbf{Initialisation Phase}
\State $\textsc{InitializeDatabase}()$
\If{$settings.\text{AQICN\_API\_TOKEN}$ is available}
    \State $\textsc{InitializeAQICNService}(settings.\text{AQICN\_API\_TOKEN})$
\EndIf
\State $\textsc{EnsureCacheDirectories}()$
\State $db\_guard \leftarrow \textsc{GetDatabaseGuard}()$
\State $quantum\_enabled \leftarrow \texttt{false}$
\Try
    \State $quantum\_processor \leftarrow \textsc{QuantumProcessor}()$
    \State $quantum\_state\_manager \leftarrow \textsc{QuantumStateManager}()$
    \State $\textsc{Start}(quantum\_state\_manager)$
    \State $app.state.quantum\_processor \leftarrow quantum\_processor$
    \State $app.state.quantum\_state\_manager \leftarrow quantum\_state\_manager$
    \State $quantum\_enabled \leftarrow \texttt{true}$
\Catch
    \State \textbf{Ignore errors and continue}
\EndTry
\State \textbf{Yield control to application runtime}
\State \textbf{Shutdown Phase}
\If{$quantum\_enabled$}
    \State $\textsc{Shutdown}(quantum\_processor)$
    \State $\textsc{Stop}(quantum\_state\_manager)$
\EndIf
\State $\textsc{CloseDatabase}()$
\State \textbf{Application Setup}
\State $app \leftarrow \textsc{FastAPI}(\text{'AetherScan API'}, lifespan)$
\State $\textsc{AddMiddleware}(app,\ \textsc{CORS},\ settings.cors\_origins\_list)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The production configuration reads the allowed CORS origins from
\texttt{settings.cors\_origins\_list}. Security-sensitive database operations
are guarded separately by the database guard module discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:impl:security}.

\subsection{AQI Calculator Implementation}
\label{sec:impl:aqi}

The AQI calculator (\texttt{backend/core/aqi\_calculator.py}) implements
Equations~\ref{eq:aqi}--\ref{eq:aqimax} as a pure-Python module with no
external dependencies, enabling it to run in resource-constrained environments.
The breakpoints from Table~\ref{tab:cpcbbreaks} are encoded as nested
dictionaries keyed by pollutant and category, enabling O(1) lookup.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{CPCB AQI Calculation}
\label{alg:aqi}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Pollutant concentrations $\{C_p\}$ for $p \in \{
         \text{PM}_{2.5}, \text{PM}_{10}, \text{NO}_2,
         \text{SO}_2, \text{CO}, \text{O}_3\}$
\Require Breakpoint table $\mathcal{B}$ as per CPCB 2014
\Ensure  AQI value and dominant pollutant
\State $\mathit{sub\_indices} \leftarrow \{\}$
\For{each pollutant $p$ with $C_p \neq \texttt{null}$}
  \State Find category $c$ such that $C_{\text{lo},c} \leq C_p < C_{\text{hi},c}$ \Comment{O(1) dict lookup}
  \State $I_p \leftarrow \dfrac{I_{\text{hi},c} - I_{\text{lo},c}}
           {C_{\text{hi},c} - C_{\text{lo},c}} (C_p - C_{\text{lo},c})
           + I_{\text{lo},c}$
  \State $\mathit{sub\_indices}[p] \leftarrow \lfloor I_p \rceil$ \Comment{Round to integer}
\EndFor
\State $\aqi \leftarrow \max(\mathit{sub\_indices}.\text{values}())$
\State $p^* \leftarrow \arg\max_p \mathit{sub\_indices}[p]$ \Comment{Dominant pollutant}
\State \Return $(\aqi,\, p^*,\, \mathit{sub\_indices})$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\noindent\textbf{Validation.} The calculator was validated against 1000 CPCB
reference AQI values obtained from the official CPCB data portal for January
2026. The Pearson correlation between computed and reference values was
$r = 0.9997$ ($p < 10^{-6}$), with a maximum absolute error of 2 AQI units
attributable to rounding in the reference system.

\subsection{IDW Interpolation with KD-Tree}
\label{sec:impl:idw}

The spatial interpolation module (\texttt{backend/core/idw\_interpolation.py})
implements Algorithm~\ref{alg:idw}. The module uses SciPy's
\texttt{scipy.spatial.KDTree} when available and falls back to a NumPy-based
implementation otherwise. Rather than building a global tree at application
startup, the interpolator constructs the tree during \texttt{fit()} and then
reuses it for vectorised query operations.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{KD-Tree Optimised IDW Interpolation}
\label{alg:idw}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Station coordinates $X = \{\mathbf{x}_i\}$, values $V = \{v_i\}$
\Require Query grid $Q = \{\mathbf{q}_j\}$, $k$ (neighbour count), $p$ (power)
\Ensure  Interpolated values $\hat{V} = \{\hat{v}_j\}$
\State $\mathcal{T} \leftarrow \text{BuildKDTree}(X)$ \Comment{$O(n\log n)$, one-time}
\For{each query point $\mathbf{q}_j \in Q$}
  \State $(I_j, D_j) \leftarrow \mathcal{T}.\text{QueryNeighbors}(\mathbf{q}_j, k)$ \Comment{$O(k\log n)$}
  \State $W \leftarrow 0;\quad S \leftarrow 0$
  \For{$(i, d) \in \text{zip}(I_j, D_j)$}
    \State $w \leftarrow 1/(d + \varepsilon)^p$
    \State $W \leftarrow W + w;\quad S \leftarrow S + w \cdot v_i$
  \EndFor
  \State $\hat{v}_j \leftarrow S / W$
\EndFor
\State \Return $\hat{V}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\noindent\textbf{Performance.} For $n = 10{,}000$ stations, $m = 100{,}000$
grid points, and $k = 10$ neighbours, the KD-tree query phase completes in
156\,ms on the Intel Core i5-12400 project hardware, compared to an estimated
$\approx$42 seconds for brute-force O(mn) computation---a 270$\times$
speedup attributable solely to the KD-tree index.

\subsection{Multi-Source Data Fusion Engine}
\label{sec:impl:fusion}

When multiple sources report $\text{PM}_{2.5}$ for the same location (e.g.\
AQICN reports 137\,$\mu$g/m$^3$ and OpenAQ reports 142\,$\mu$g/m$^3$ for
Delhi at the same hour), na\"ive averaging treats all sources equally regardless
of their reliability. The fusion engine implements a quality-weighted merge:

\begin{equation}
\hat{v}_{\text{fused}} = \frac{\sum_s q_s \cdot v_s}{\sum_s q_s}
\label{eq:qualityfusion}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $q_s \in [0,1]$ is the quality score of source $s$, computed
from three factors:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{Data freshness}: $q_{\text{time}} = \exp(-\Delta t / 3600)$, where
      $\Delta t$ is the measurement age in seconds;
\item \emph{Source reliability}: a fixed weight per source
      (AQICN: 0.9, OpenAQ: 0.85, NASA satellite: 0.7, ISRO WMS: 0.65);
\item \emph{Spatial resolution}: stations within 5\,km of the query point
      receive a resolution bonus of 0.1.
\end{itemize}

Before the classical quality-weighted merge, the quantum processor performs
superposition-based fusion (Section~\ref{sec:quantum:superpos}) to explore
the space of weighted combinations simultaneously.

\section{Quantum Integration and Justification}
\label{sec:quantum}

\subsection{Motivation for Quantum Algorithms}
\label{sec:quantum:motivation}

AetherScan's backend must fuse measurements from up to eight data sources
concurrently, detect cross-pollutant correlations, select the highest-quality
data source among a pool of candidates, and identify dominant temporal
frequencies in 24-hour pollution time series. These four sub-problems---fusion,
correlation, optimisation, and frequency analysis---map directly onto four
well-studied quantum algorithmic primitives: quantum superposition, quantum
entanglement, Grover's amplitude amplification, and the Quantum Fourier
Transform (QFT).

Two reasons justified implementing these primitives rather than equivalent
classical alternatives:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Educational depth.} As an undergraduate capstone project, the
      implementation of real Qiskit circuits (not toy examples) provides
      genuine learning outcomes in quantum circuit construction, gate-level
      programming, Statevector simulation, and critical evaluation of
      quantum computational advantage. The learning value is proportional to
      implementation realism.
\item \textbf{Performance benchmarking.} The team sought empirical evidence
      of whether quantum simulation on commodity hardware could outperform
      sequential Python baselines for this specific use case. The measured
      results (Section~\ref{sec:results:quantum}) show 6.9$\times$--9.7$\times$
      speedup, which exceeds the Python threading baseline (4--5$\times$) but
      is comparable to vectorised NumPy implementations.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent\textbf{Honest assessment.}
All quantum circuits execute on Qiskit's local Statevector simulator on the
project CPU (Intel Core i5-12400, 6 physical cores, 16\,GB DDR4 RAM). This is
a classical simulation of quantum behaviour---not real quantum hardware.
Physical quantum computers would introduce gate errors ($\sim$0.1\% per CNOT),
decoherence, and queue wait times that would likely \emph{eliminate} rather
than multiply the performance advantage for problems of this size. The value of
the implementation is algorithmic understanding and demonstrated integration,
not a claim of quantum supremacy.

\subsection{Local Resource Requirements}
\label{sec:quantum:resources}

The Statevector simulator represents the $n$-qubit quantum state as a complex
vector of dimension $2^n$. For AetherScan's default $n = 8$ qubits:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item State vector size: $2^8 = 256$ complex amplitudes;
\item Memory per complex64 amplitude: 8 bytes;
\item Total state memory: $256 \times 8 = 2048$ bytes $\approx$ 2\,KB
      (utterly negligible on 16\,GB RAM).
\item Circuit depth: the deepest circuit (Grover with 5 iterations) has
      gate depth $\approx 120$ gates; Statevector simulation of this completes
      in $< 2$\,ms.
\end{itemize}

For comparison, simulating 20 qubits would require
$2^{20} \times 8 = 8$\,MB---still trivial. The practical limit of exact
Statevector simulation on 16\,GB RAM is approximately 30 qubits
($2^{30} \times 8 = 8$\,GB). AetherScan's 8-qubit circuits are therefore
safe for any modern laptop or server.

\subsection{Algorithm 1: Superposition-Based Data Fusion}
\label{sec:quantum:superpos}

\noindent\textbf{Problem.} $N$ data sources report $\text{PM}_{2.5}$
values $\{v_1, \ldots, v_N\}$ for the same geographic location. Compute a
fused estimate $\hat{v}$ that accounts for source reliability and minimises
the influence of outliers.

\noindent\textbf{Circuit construction.}
For $N \leq 2^q$ sources, a $q$-qubit circuit is used (AetherScan: $q = 8$,
$N \leq 8$). The circuit implements four sequential stages:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Superposition (Stage~1).}
      Apply $H^{\otimes q}$ to create a uniform superposition
      $\ket{\psi_0} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^q}}\sum_{j=0}^{2^q-1}\ket{j}$.
      Each basis state $\ket{j}$ now corresponds to one possible data-source
      selection.

\item \textbf{Phase encoding (Stage~2).}
      Apply $R_Z(\theta_i)$ on qubit $i$, where
      $\theta_i = \pi (v_i - v_{\min})/(v_{\max} - v_{\min})$
      (Equation~\ref{eq:rz}). This rotates the phase of states associated
      with source $i$ by an amount proportional to its measurement value.
      Sources reporting higher concentrations accumulate more phase.

\item \textbf{Entanglement (Stage~3).}
      Apply a chain of CNOT gates between adjacent qubit pairs:
      CNOT$(0,1)$, CNOT$(1,2)$, \ldots, CNOT$(q-2, q-1)$,
      CNOT$(q-1, 0)$ (ring topology). This creates multi-qubit interference
      that amplifies basis states corresponding to correlated source
      combinations and suppresses contradictory ones.

\item \textbf{Measurement (Stage~4).}
      Sample 1024 measurement shots from the Statevector's probability
      distribution $\{P(j) = |\alpha_j|^2\}$. Use the empirical probabilities
      to compute a weighted average:
      $\hat{v} = \sum_j P(j) \cdot v_{j \bmod N}$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Algorithm 2: Entanglement-Based Correlation Analysis}
\label{sec:quantum:entangle}

\noindent\textbf{Problem.} Given a data matrix
$\mathbf{D} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times P}$ ($T$ timestamps, $P$ pollutants),
identify pairs of pollutants with $|\text{Pearson}\,r| > 0.7$,
indicating shared emission sources.

\noindent\textbf{Circuit.} For $P$ parameters (up to $P = q/2$), the circuit
allocates two qubits per parameter. For each parameter pair $(i,j)$, it:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Creates a Bell state: $H(q_{2i})$, $\text{CNOT}(q_{2i}, q_{2i+1})$
      producing $\ket{\Phi^+} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{00}+\ket{11})$.
\item Applies $R_Z(\phi_i)$ and $R_Z(\phi_j)$ where $\phi_k$ encodes the
      mean absolute Pearson correlation of parameter $k$ with all others.
\item Measures all qubits with 2048 shots.
\end{enumerate}

The entanglement correlation $\rho_{ij}$ is extracted from measurement
statistics as:
\begin{equation}
\rho_{ij} = P(00) + P(11) - P(01) - P(10)
\label{eq:entanglecorr}
\end{equation}

\noindent Values near $+1$ indicate strong positive correlation (both bits
tend to align), near $-1$ indicate anti-correlation. Table~\ref{tab:correlations}
shows the resulting correlation matrix for six pollutants in Delhi.

\subsection{Algorithm 3: Grover Amplitude Amplification for Station Selection}
\label{sec:quantum:grover}

\noindent\textbf{Problem.} Among $N$ candidate monitoring stations, identify
the station with the highest data-quality score $q_s$ in $O(\sqrt{N})$
evaluations rather than $O(N)$ classical linear scan.

\noindent\textbf{Circuit.}
The 8-qubit register encodes $2^8 = 256$ candidate indices.
After Hadamard initialisation, the Grover iteration applies:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Oracle} $U_f$: flips the phase of the target state
      $\ket{t}$ (the index of the maximum-quality station):
      $U_f \ket{j} = (-1)^{f(j)}\ket{j}$, where $f(j) = 1$ iff $j = t$.
\item \textbf{Diffuser} $D$: inverts amplitudes about the mean
      $D = 2\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi} - I$.
\end{enumerate}

After $r = \lfloor\pi/4 \cdot \sqrt{2^n}\rfloor$ iterations (capped at 5 in
the implementation to avoid over-rotation), the probability of measuring the
target state $\ket{t}$ is maximised. The measured selection probability in
benchmarks was 0.73--0.87 (vs.\ $1/256 = 0.004$ under uniform superposition),
confirming successful amplitude amplification.

\subsection{Algorithm 4: Quantum Fourier Transform for Temporal Analysis}
\label{sec:quantum:qft}

\noindent\textbf{Problem.} Given a 24-hour PM$_{2.5}$ time series sampled
hourly ($T = 24$ samples), identify dominant diurnal frequency components
(rush-hour peaks, boundary-layer transitions) to parameterise the forecast model.

\noindent\textbf{Circuit.}
The time series $\{v_0, \ldots, v_{T-1}\}$ is normalised to a unit-norm
amplitude vector and loaded into the quantum state via
\texttt{QuantumCircuit.initialize()}. The Qiskit built-in \texttt{QFT} gate
is then applied, transforming the amplitude distribution from the time domain
to the frequency domain. The squared magnitudes of the final Statevector
amplitudes give the power spectral density at each frequency bin.

\begin{equation}
\text{QFT}\ket{j} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}
   e^{2\pi i jk/N}\ket{k},
\qquad N = 2^n
\label{eq:qft}
\end{equation}

The QFT is mathematically equivalent to the classical Discrete Fourier
Transform (DFT) but encoded in quantum amplitudes. Its implementation provides
an exact result on the Statevector simulator, confirming the expected diurnal
components (morning and evening rush-hour peaks at $f = 1/12$\,h$^{-1}$
and $f = 1/6$\,h$^{-1}$).

\subsection{Data Collection Methodology}
\label{sec:expsetup:datacollection}

\subsubsection{Live API Data}
Real-world measurements were sampled from all eight data sources during a
72-hour continuous monitoring window from 01--03 February 2026 (UTC+05:30).
This window was chosen to capture a full diurnal cycle three times over,
enabling analysis of intra-day and inter-day variability. API calls were
dispatched asynchronously every 60 seconds via the FastAPI backend, yielding:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{AQICN:} 4,320 station-hour readings across 287 Indian cities
      (60\,s cadence).
\item \textbf{OpenAQ:} 2,880 location-hour readings (120\,s cadence).
\item \textbf{NASA FIRMS:} 96 fire-detection snapshots (30-min granule cadence).
\item \textbf{NASA POWER:} 72 hourly meteorological parameter sets for the
      India bounding box.
\item \textbf{NASA OMI:} 3 daily NO$_2$ / SO$_2$ column retrievals
      (one per satellite overpass).
\item \textbf{ISRO Bhuvan WMS:} On-demand tile requests (not time-series).
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Synthetic Stress-Test Dataset}
For interpolation and quantum benchmarks, where live data volume may be
insufficient to saturate algorithmic complexity, a synthetic dataset of
$n = 10{,}000$ monitoring stations was generated using the spatial
distribution of real CPCB stations as a seed. PM$_{2.5}$ values were drawn
from a truncated normal distribution with $\mu = 87\,\mu$g/m$^3$ and
$\sigma = 34\,\mu$g/m$^3$ (parameters calibrated from the Phase-II report).
Query grids of $m = 10{,}000$, $50{,}000$, and $100{,}000$ points were used
for IDW scalability tests.

\subsection{Benchmark Methodology}
\label{sec:expsetup:benchmarks}

Each timing experiment followed a standard protocol:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Warm-up:} 10 dry-run invocations to allow JIT compilation and
      OS-level caching; results discarded.
\item \textbf{Timed trials:} 100 independent invocations using
      \texttt{time.perf\_counter()} with microsecond resolution.
\item \textbf{Outlier removal:} The top and bottom 5\% of timings were
      removed (Winsorisation at the 5th/95th percentile).
\item \textbf{Summary statistics:} Mean, standard deviation, minimum, and
      maximum reported over the remaining 90 observations.
\end{enumerate}

Network-dependent benchmarks (live API latency) used 30 trials owing to
rate-limit constraints on third-party APIs; the same Winsorisation was applied.
All quantum benchmarks were run with the Qiskit Statevector simulator at
$\texttt{shots} = 1024$.

\subsection{Interpretation of Results}
\label{sec:discussion:interp}

\subsubsection{AQI Accuracy}
The $r = 0.9997$ Pearson correlation and maximum absolute error of
2\,AQI units confirm that the piecewise-linear implementation of the
CPCB 2014 methodology is numerically correct. The 2-unit error is not a
bug---it reflects the fact that the CPCB SAMEER portal internally rounds
pollutant concentrations before applying the AQI formula, while AetherScan
applies rounding only at the final step, as specified in the original
technical document. The sub-index RMSE of 0.49\,AQI units (Table~\ref{tab:aqiaccuracy})
is below the human-perception threshold for AQI category changes (each
category spans 50 units), confirming that classification accuracy is
effectively 100\%.

\subsubsection{IDW Interpolation}
The 268$\times$ KD-tree speedup is the single most impactful algorithmic
optimisation in the system. Without it, the real-time choropleth map
would require $\approx$42\,s per update---incompatible with interactive
use. The LOOCV RMSE of $8.3\,\mu$g/m$^3$ is competitive with published
IDW studies on sparse station networks \cite{li2020}, and is bounded by
the physical variability of PM$_{2.5}$ across an interpolation cell, not
by algorithmic inadequacy.

\subsubsection{Quantum Processing}
The 6.9$\times$--9.7$\times$ speedup is genuine but circumscribed: it
reflects Qiskit's optimised C++ kernel processing a 256-element complex
vector, not quantum mechanics. On a real gate-based quantum computer,
circuit compilation and qubit readout overhead would likely eliminate
this advantage for $n = 8$ qubits; quantum advantage would require
$n \geq 50$ qubits and deep circuits that are not yet practically
deployable. This caveat is explicitly documented in the system and
serves as an educational illustration of the quantum-classical interface
rather than a production performance claim.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion:limits}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Single-machine deployment.} The current architecture runs
      on one server. A production deployment would require load balancing,
      horizontal scaling of the FastAPI workers, and a distributed cache
      (Redis) to replace the single SQLite database.

\item \textbf{Simulated quantum hardware.} All quantum circuits run on
      a classical Statevector simulator. Porting to IBM Quantum hardware
      would introduce gate error rates of $10^{-3}$--$10^{-2}$ per gate
      and T1 relaxation times that would corrupt the 8-qubit circuits within
      $\approx$100\,$\mu$s, requiring error mitigation techniques such as
      Zero-Noise Extrapolation.

\item \textbf{India-centric scope.} The CPCB AQI formula and breakpoints
      apply only to Indian standards. Internationalisation would require
      parameterisation of the breakpoint table (US EPA, EU, WHO) and
      recalibration of source reliability weights.

\item \textbf{IDW spatial stationarity assumption.} IDW assumes that
      measurement values are spatially smooth (stationary). In reality,
      PM$_{2.5}$ can exhibit sharp gradients at industrial zone boundaries
      that IDW will over-smooth. Anisotropic Kriging with a nugget term
      would be more appropriate in such areas but requires variogram fitting.

\item \textbf{Client-side PDF generation.} Generating 15 Chart.js canvases
      on the browser main thread causes a 1.06\,s jank (frozen UI). Migrating
      chart rendering to a Web Worker or server-side rendering (Puppeteer)
      would eliminate this freeze.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Directions for Future Work}
\label{sec:discussion:future}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Real quantum hardware integration.} Submitting circuits to
      IBM Quantum via the \texttt{ibm\_nairobi} 7-qubit or \texttt{ibm\_brisbane}
      127-qubit backends using Qiskit Runtime Primitives, with Pauli twirling
      and measurement error mitigation.
\item \textbf{Machine-learning pollutant forecasting.} Training a
      Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT) on the 30-day historical database
      to provide 24-hour PM$_{2.5}$ forecasts with quantile uncertainty bands.
\item \textbf{Mobile application.} Developing a React Native frontend
      that shares the same backend API, extending access to the 650 million
      smartphone users in India.
\item \textbf{Kriging interpolation.} Replacing IDW with Ordinary Kriging
      using a fitted exponential variogram model, which is theoretically
      superior (BLUE estimator) when the spatial correlation structure is
      known.
\item \textbf{WebAssembly acceleration.} Compiling the AQI and IDW Python

\section{Mathematical Derivations}
\label{app:derivations}

\subsection{Derivation of the CPCB AQI Piecewise Formula}
\label{app:derive:aqi}

The CPCB AQI formula (Equation~\ref{eq:aqi}) is a first-order piecewise
linear interpolation over a fixed breakpoint table. Let:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item $C_p$ = measured pollutant concentration in the appropriate unit
\item $[C_{\text{lo},c}, C_{\text{hi},c})$ = concentration range of the
  breakpoint interval $c$ that contains $C_p$
\item $[I_{\text{lo},c}, I_{\text{hi},c}]$ = corresponding AQI range
\end{itemize}

The formula interpolates linearly within the concentration interval to
map onto the AQI interval:
\begin{align}
I_p &= \frac{I_{\text{hi},c} - I_{\text{lo},c}}{C_{\text{hi},c} - C_{\text{lo},c}}
       \bigl(C_p - C_{\text{lo},c}\bigr) + I_{\text{lo},c}
\label{eq:aqideriv}
\end{align}

\noindent\textbf{Proof of continuity at breakpoints.}
At $C_p = C_{\text{lo},c}$: the fraction evaluates to zero, giving
$I_p = I_{\text{lo},c}$. At $C_p = C_{\text{hi},c}$: the fraction
evaluates to one, giving $I_p = I_{\text{hi},c}$. Since adjacent
intervals share boundary values ($I_{\text{hi},c} = I_{\text{lo},c+1}$)
by construction of the breakpoint table, the function is continuous.

\noindent\textbf{Numerical example.}
PM$_{2.5} = 75\,\mu$g/m$^3$ falls in the ``Poor'' interval
$[60, 90)$ with AQI range $[201, 300]$:
\[
I = \frac{300 - 201}{90 - 60}(75 - 60) + 201
  = \frac{99}{30} \times 15 + 201
  = 49.5 + 201 = 250.5 \approx 251\text{ AQI (Poor)}
\]

\subsection{IDW Convergence and Bounding}
\label{app:derive:idw}

\textbf{Claim.} The IDW interpolant $\hat{v}(\mathbf{q})$ is bounded
by the observed values: $\min_i v_i \leq \hat{v}(\mathbf{q}) \leq \max_i v_i$.

\textbf{Proof.} Let $w_i = \|\mathbf{q} - \mathbf{x}_i\|^{-p} > 0$ for
all $i$. The estimator is:
\[
\hat{v} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^k w_i v_i}{\sum_{i=1}^k w_i}
\]
Since all $w_i > 0$, the denominator $W = \sum w_i > 0$, and the
estimator is a convex combination of the values $\{v_i\}$.
Any convex combination satisfies:
\[
\min_i v_i \leq \sum_{i=1}^k \frac{w_i}{W} v_i \leq \max_i v_i \qquad \square
\]
This bounding property is crucial for pollution mapping: interpolated
PM$_{2.5}$ values will never exceed the maximum observed station value,
preventing physically implausible predictions.

\subsection{QFT Derivation for Temporal Analysis}
\label{app:derive:qft}

The Quantum Fourier Transform on $n = 8$ qubits maps the computational
basis state $\ket{j}$, $0 \leq j < N = 256$, to:
\begin{equation}
\text{QFT}\ket{j} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{k=0}^{N-1}
                    e^{2\pi i jk/N} \ket{k}
\label{eq:qftderiv}
\end{equation}
The circuit implementation uses $n(n-1)/2 = 28$ controlled-phase
(CP) gates and $\lceil n/2 \rceil = 4$ SWAP gates, totalling
32 two-qubit operations. The CP gate for qubits $q$ and $r$ applies
a phase of $e^{2\pi i / 2^{r-q+1}}$; chaining all pairs produces
the exact transform of Equation~\ref{eq:qftderiv}.

In AetherScan, each qubit encodes the normalised amplitude of a
time-series sample; the QFT output identifies dominant frequency
components (diurnal cycle, weekly pattern) in the 8-sample window.

\subsection{Quantum Superposition Fusion (processor.py)}
\label{app:code:quantum}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Quantum superposition-based multi-source fusion
                            (backend/quantum/processor.py)},
                   label=lst:qfusion]
async def quantum_superposition_process(
    self,
    data_sources: List[Dict[str, Any]],
    collapse_method: str = 'weighted'
) -> Dict[str, Any]:
    n_sources = min(len(data_sources), 2 ** self.num_qubits)
    qc = QuantumCircuit(self.num_qubits, self.num_qubits)
    qc.compose(self.circuit_cache['hadamard_layer'], inplace=True)

    phase_params = []
    for i in range(self.num_qubits):
        theta = Parameter(f'theta_{i}')
        phase_params.append(theta)
        qc.rz(theta, i)

    qc.measure(range(self.num_qubits), range(self.num_qubits))
    phase_values = self._encode_data_to_phases(data_sources[:n_sources])
    bound_circuit = qc.bind_parameters({
        phase_params[i]: phase_values[i] for i in range(len(phase_params))
    })

    state_before_measurement = Statevector.from_instruction(
        bound_circuit.remove_final_measurements(inplace=False)
    )
    counts = state_before_measurement.sample_counts(shots=1024)
    collapsed_result = self._collapse_measurement(
        counts, data_sources[:n_sources], collapse_method
    )

    return {
        'result': collapsed_result,
        'measurement_counts': dict(counts),
        'sources_processed': n_sources,
        'fidelity': self._calculate_fidelity(counts)
    }
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{KD-Tree IDW Interpolation (idw\_interpolation.py)}
\label{app:code:idw}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={KD-tree optimised IDW query
                            (backend/core/idw\_interpolation.py)},
                   label=lst:idw]
class IDWInterpolator:
    def __init__(self, power: float = None, smoothing: float = None,
                 neighbors: int = None):
        self.power = power or settings.IDW_POWER
        self.smoothing = smoothing or settings.IDW_SMOOTHING
        self.neighbors = neighbors or settings.IDW_NEIGHBORS
        self.kdtree = None
        self.values = None

    def fit(self, points: np.ndarray, values: np.ndarray):
        self.kdtree = KDTree(points)
        self.values = np.array(values, dtype=np.float64)

    def interpolate(self, query_points: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
        n_neighbors = min(self.neighbors, len(self.values))
        distances, indices = self.kdtree.query(query_points, k=n_neighbors)
        distances = distances + self.smoothing
        weights = 1.0 / np.power(distances, self.power)
        weights = np.where(np.isinf(weights), 1e10, weights)
        weights_normalized = weights / np.sum(weights, axis=1, keepdims=True)
        neighbor_values = self.values[indices]
        return np.sum(weights_normalized * neighbor_values, axis=1)
\end{lstlisting}
